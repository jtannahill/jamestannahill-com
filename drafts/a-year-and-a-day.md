# A Year and a Day

## On what a free society will let you walk away from, and why the powerful would rather write a cheque

There is a detail in the history of medieval Europe that has always seemed to me more instructive than most of what is written about liberty in the abstract. Under the customary law of the German towns, a serf who reached a chartered city and lived there unclaimed for a year and a day ceased to be a serf. Stadtluft macht frei nach Jahr und Tag. City air makes you free, after a year and a day.

What is worth noticing is the mechanism. Nobody enfranchised the serf. No one improved his condition, raised his wages, or acknowledged his dignity. No lord underwent a change of heart, and no philosopher persuaded anyone of anything. The serf simply became, as a practical matter, retrievable at a cost the lord was unwilling to pay, and at the end of that period the law declined to help him. The whole of the transformation lay in the existence of somewhere else to go and a rule that made going there stick.

I raise this because we are now several years into an enthusiasm for a different mechanism, and the enthusiasm has a quality that ought to arouse more curiosity than it has.

The proposal to guarantee every citizen an unconditional income has moved, in under a decade, from the margins of economics to the standard furniture of the technology executive's public philosophy. It has been tested in Stockton, across Finland, and in rural Kenya. It has been endorsed, funded, and in some cases personally underwritten by men whose fortunes derive from the automation of the labour it is meant to replace. The arguments in its favour are serious, the pilot data are more encouraging than its critics admit, and I do not intend to dispute either.

I intend to ask a narrower question. Why is this the concession that gets offered?

## The affordability of generosity

A useful habit, when a powerful party volunteers a sacrifice, is to establish the currency in which the sacrifice is denominated. Money is the most fungible thing a firm possesses and by some distance the least dangerous thing it can part with. A payment is an expense. It appears on a line, it is provisioned for, and it is recovered through prices, through productivity, or through the tax base. Nothing about a payment alters the relation between the party making it and the party receiving it, which is precisely what recommends it.

Consider what is not on offer with anything like the same enthusiasm. Employers who will discuss a wage floor at length will litigate for years over a non-compete. Platforms that will fund creators generously will not make an audience portable, because the audience is the asset and the creator is a tenant on it. Firms that will match a pension contribution will structure the vesting so that departure in year three forfeits it. States that will pay a family allowance will not make a residency permit separable from the employer who sponsored it.

The pattern is consistent enough to be worth naming. What is conceded is money. What is withheld is the ability to leave.

The two are not alternatives, and I want to be careful not to pretend they are. But they address different constraints, and only one of them addresses the binding one.

## What exit actually costs

The case for an income floor as a source of bargaining power runs as follows: a person who can eat without working can refuse bad work, and the refusal, aggregated, disciplines employers. This is correct, and it is the strongest argument in the proposal's favour. My objection is that it describes a real mechanism operating at the wrong margin.

A subsistence floor raises the reservation wage at the very bottom of the labour market, where the alternative to a job is destitution. It does considerably less for the person whose captivity is the ordinary kind. Consider the constraints that actually hold a mid-career professional in place. Health insurance attached to employment. A visa whose validity is the property of the sponsoring firm. A non-compete covering the only industry in which fifteen years of accumulated judgement is worth anything. Deferred compensation with a cliff eighteen months out. An occupational licence that does not travel across a state line. A mortgage underwritten against a specific salary at a specific employer. A professional reputation held inside a network that the employer curates.

None of these is a price. Each is a permission. Cash addresses prices. Only law and institutional design address permissions, and the distinction matters because the two are not substitutes at any exchange rate. There is no sum of money that converts a non-compete into a nullity or a sponsored visa into a right of residence. The captive is not poor. He is held.

The American case is particularly clean, because the central instrument of it was an accident. During the Second World War, with wages frozen, the National War Labor Board determined in 1943 that employer contributions to health and pension plans did not constitute wages for the purposes of the freeze. Firms competing for scarce labour therefore competed in benefits, the arrangement was subsequently blessed by the tax code, and a country acquired, without ever deciding to, a system in which medical care for a family of four is contingent on one adult's continued good standing with one employer. It would be difficult to design a more effective constraint on labour mobility if one set out to do it deliberately, and it is worth observing that nobody did. The most powerful exit barrier in the American economy is a piece of wartime accounting that was never repealed.

## The evidence from an institution nobody wanted to weaken

The objection that arrives at this point is that a right of departure is a solvent, and that a society which guarantees everyone the capacity to leave will find that its institutions do not survive the guarantee. This is a serious objection and I think the honest answer is empirical.

We have run the experiment. Between the late nineteen sixties and the mid nineteen eighties, most American states moved from divorce requiring the demonstration of fault to divorce available at the unilateral election of either party. This was, at the time, understood by thoughtful people as a straightforward weakening of an institution, and in one narrow sense it was.

The consequences inside marriages that did not end are the interesting part. Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers, examining the state-by-state variation in the timing of these reforms, found that the introduction of unilateral divorce was followed by substantial declines in female suicide, in domestic violence, and in spousal homicide. The mechanism is not obscure. The credible availability of departure changes the terms of every negotiation conducted in its shadow, including the great majority of negotiations that end in the parties staying together. The wife who could leave was treated differently from the wife who could not, and she was treated differently whether or not she left.

This is the whole of the argument in one finding. An exit right does its most important work on behalf of the people who never exercise it. It is not principally a mechanism of escape. It is a mechanism of standing, and standing is a good that no transfer payment purchases, because standing is constituted by what the other party knows you are capable of doing.

## The measure of a regime

The historical version of this proposition is old enough that we have stopped hearing it. Hume observed that Europe's political fragmentation, which looked like a weakness, disciplined its sovereigns, because a prince who taxed or persecuted beyond a certain point discovered that his artisans and his lenders and his scholars had legs. The rivalry of the European states was not a marketplace of ideas in the genteel sense. It was a marketplace in the literal sense, and the commodity was residents.

The inverse holds with even greater regularity. A regime that must physically prevent departure has already conceded the argument about its own legitimacy, and it has conceded it in a form no propaganda can retract. This is why the Berlin Wall was such an efficient piece of political rhetoric on behalf of the people who did not build it. Nothing said about the German Democratic Republic by its enemies was half so damaging as the fortifications it maintained against its own citizens. The drafters of the Universal Declaration understood the point well enough to place the right to leave any country, including one's own, in Article 13, and it remains the article that authoritarian governments find least congenial and most necessary to violate quietly.

We are willing to apply this test to states. We are, for some reason, unwilling to apply it to firms, to platforms, and to the other institutions that now structure the greater part of a life. The question of what an arrangement costs to leave is treated as a matter of contract in the one case and as a matter of fundamental liberty in the other, and I have never seen the distinction defended, only assumed.

## What ought not to be cheap

Here I must part company with the libertarian version of this argument, which holds that exit costs are simply a friction and that a free society minimises them.

That is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that matters. Some exit costs are intrinsic to the goods they attach to. Leaving a marriage ought to be painful, because a marriage that could be dissolved without cost would not be the thing we are trying to protect. An apprenticeship that could be abandoned in week three at no loss would not transmit a craft. A fiduciary duty that evaporated on resignation would not be a duty. Citizenship that carried no obligation would not be citizenship, and a country of perfectly frictionless residents is not a country but an airport.

Loyalty is a virtue, and a virtue exercised at no cost is not being exercised at all. Any argument for exit that cannot distinguish the sunk devotion of a long attachment from a fence erected by an interested party has proved too much and should be discarded.

The distinction I want is between costs that inhere in the good and costs that are engineered by the party that benefits from your captivity. The grief of leaving a vocation is the first kind. A non-compete is the second. The loss of a community is the first kind. An employer-tied insurance policy is the second. The test is not whether departure is expensive. The test is who set the price, and whether they set it for their own account.

Albert Hirschman, who gave us the vocabulary of exit and voice more than fifty years ago, warned about the other failure mode, and it is the strongest thing that can be said against me. Where exit is too easy, voice atrophies. The able leave rather than reform, the institution loses precisely the constituency with the capacity to repair it, and decline becomes orderly and terminal. Anyone who has watched a good firm hollow out through the quiet departure of the people who might have fixed it has seen this happen.

I take the point and I think it constrains the conclusion rather than defeating it. What is wanted is not costless exit but available exit: an option that is genuinely open, whose exercise remains a decision with weight. The serf in the town had to survive a year and a day. That is not nothing. It is simply not the lord's to set.

## The cheque and what it buys

Which returns us to the enthusiasm.

I do not believe that the technologists funding basic income pilots are engaged in a deception, and the conspiratorial reading of their motives is both unfair and unnecessary. Something duller is going on, and duller explanations are usually the correct ones. They are proposing the remedy that is legible to them, denominated in the units they possess in abundance, addressed to the problem as it appears from where they are standing. From that vantage the difficulty of the coming decades looks like a shortfall of income, because income is the thing they can see going missing from the models.

But the loss will not principally be a loss of income, and the transfer will not principally be a transfer of power. A person whose labour has become surplus does not merely stop being paid. He stops being someone whose departure would be noticed, and it is that, and not the money, which will prove to be the injury. To be paid to remain is not the same as being needed, and everyone on both ends of such an arrangement understands the difference immediately, whatever either party says about it.

There is a reason the medieval formula concerned residence and not relief. Alms were available in the manor. What the town offered was somewhere else to be, and a rule that made the somewhere else hold.

The measure of a free society is not what it will give you. It is what it will let you walk away from, and the striking willingness of the powerful to discuss the first while declining to discuss the second is the most reliable evidence we have of which one is actually worth having.
